Stolen or guessed passwords are the most common way into other people's accounts. A second factor bound to a device or a key makes a captured password worthless on its own. IT-Grundschutz asks for strong authentication above all where accounts are reachable from the internet or hold special rights (module ORP.4).

## What matters

- **The risky ways in first:** administration, remote access, cloud services, then everyone.
- **Phishing-resistant methods** such as FIDO2 keys or sign-ins bound to the device also protect against fake sign-in pages.
- **Closing gaps:** switch off legacy protocols that bypass multi-factor sign-in.
- **Break-glass accounts without a gap:** two accounts excluded from the policy keep a faulty policy from locking everyone out. Microsoft recommends passkeys (FIDO2) or certificate-based authentication for them and advises excluding them from restricting policies. Registering a method enforces nothing by itself, though: Microsoft requires MFA for these accounts too only at its admin portals and tools; elsewhere a captured password can be enough. Weigh this up and record it: either follow Microsoft for both accounts and limit the remaining risk by having every sign-in reported, or deviate on purpose: one account stays free of every policy, and for the second a policy of its own requires a phishing-resistant method. The key and a long password are kept apart in the safe, every sign-in with them is reported, and the sign-in is tested regularly.
- **Password manager:** the organisation provides one, with an encrypted database and access by master password or MFA (Grundschutz++, BER.6.1; the requirement leaves open where it runs). Only the Edition 2023 (ORP.4.A8) rules out functions that synchronise passwords through third-party online services or send them to third parties, end-to-end encryption included; it therefore leads to a local or self-hosted manager.
- **Evidence:** the policy and a report of which accounts still lack a second factor.
